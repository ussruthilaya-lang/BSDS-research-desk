# 4. Related Work

**4. Related work.** Research Desk draws on two bodies of work. The first is the course material on failure recovery, event ordering and shared work. The second is recent practice in multi-agent research systems. These systems are the workload that Research Desk runs underneath.

**Course readings.**

- **MapReduce** (J. Dean and S. Ghemawat, *Communications of the ACM*, 2008; HW2 Part I).
  - The master re-runs the completed map tasks of a failed worker, because their output is only on its local disk. It does not re-run completed reduce tasks, whose output is already in the distributed file system. Research Desk follows the same rule. A subagent saves each step's result, so a crash repeats only the unfinished step. The cost differs. A subagent step spends a call from a shared budget, so experiment E4 counts a repeated step as a duplicate call.
- **Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System** (L. Lamport, *Communications of the ACM*, July 1978, pp. 558-565; HW3 Part I).
  - Separate machines share no clock, and "happened before" is the only order that events have for certain. In Research Desk, deadlines travel over HTTP as remaining time. Times in shared state come from one clock, the one in Redis. Only the gateway's clock paces calls.
- **Distributed Systems for Fun and Profit** (M. Takada; recommended text). Most real systems assume crash-recovery: a node fails only by crashing, and it may come back later. Research Desk assumes this for its workers, and the leases and per-step checkpoints are built for it. It does not handle Byzantine faults (nodes that send wrong answers) and does not use a consensus protocol such as Paxos or Raft. That is future work.

**Outside material.**

- **How we built our multi-agent research system** (Anthropic Engineering, June 13, 2025; https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system).
  - A lead agent starts 3-5 subagents in parallel. Early subagents "performed the exact same searches as other agents". The post says "Agents are stateful and errors compound" and describes systems that "can resume from where the agent was when the errors occurred". It does not mention provider rate limits. Research Desk treats such a system as the workload. It studies one shared budget, reuse across subagents, and recovery of a crashed subagent's work.
- **arXiv API terms of use** (https://info.arxiv.org/help/api/tou.html). For the legacy APIs the limit is "no more than one request every three seconds", with a single connection at a time. It applies to "all of the machines under your control as a whole". The simulated provider models these limits. The project never calls arXiv. The page says the limits may change.
- **singleflight** (Go package `golang.org/x/sync/singleflight`; https://pkg.go.dev/golang.org/x/sync/singleflight). It is "A duplicate function call suppression mechanism", and its group lives inside one program. Research Desk merges identical in-flight searches across several worker machines, through shared state. The merged request keeps the latest waiter's deadline. The claim on a search expires, so a dead claimant cannot block the waiters.
- **The Tail at Scale** (J. Dean and L. A. Barroso, *Communications of the ACM*, 56, 2013, pp. 74-80; https://research.google/pubs/the-tail-at-scale/). At large scale, short high-latency episodes "may come to dominate overall service performance". Only the abstract was read. It motivates experiment E3, which asks what one slow subagent costs the whole answer, and the gateway's deadline check, which uses a 95th-percentile latency estimate.
- **Model Context Protocol, architecture overview** (https://modelcontextprotocol.io/docs/2026-07-28/learn/architecture.md). Local servers on the stdio transport "typically serve a single MCP client". Remote servers on the Streamable HTTP transport "will typically serve many MCP clients". The public service uses Streamable HTTP, because many users share it.

**Position.** The related work covers the agents on top (Anthropic) and the failure and ordering ideas underneath (MapReduce, Lamport, Takada). None of the sources measures what a shared, rate-limited provider budget does to a fleet of subagents. Research Desk adds a small, repeatable study of that layer. It measures how four parts change throughput, cost and recovery when every provider call is metered: a cache, merging of identical searches, a paced gateway with admission control, and task leases.
